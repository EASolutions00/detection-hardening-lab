Proposed Title
Automated Analytic-Robustness Scoring of Sigma and Wazuh Detection Rules Using a Rule-Feature Dependency Model Based on the Summiting the Pyramid MethodologyArea of Investigation
          The study falls under the field of cybersecurity, specifically within the sub-disciplines of security operations, detection engineering, and the measurement of detection quality. Security operations refers to the continuous practice of monitoring, detecting, investigating, and responding to malicious activity across an organization's computing infrastructure, a function typically institutionalized in a Security Operations Center (SOC). Defenders in this setting do not observe attacker activity directly; they observe it through detection rules, which are declarative statements describing the observable characteristics of malicious behavior and which generate an alert whenever incoming log data satisfies those characteristics.
          Detection rules are expressed in platform-specific languages, a fragmentation that led to the development of Sigma, an open and vendor-neutral rule format written in YAML Ain't Markup Language (YAML) that can be translated into the query languages of individual monitoring platforms. The publicly maintained SigmaHQ repository has accumulated several thousand community-contributed rules and has become a common reference corpus for the discipline. Platforms such as Wazuh, an open-source Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) and Extended Detection and Response (XDR) system, maintain their own rule formats while participating in the same ecosystem through rule conversion. The authorship and maintenance of this content has matured into a discipline known as detection engineering, which treats detection logic as a software artifact subject to version control, peer review, testing, and continuous integration, a practice commonly described as detection-as-code.
          The adversary behaviors that detection rules are written to catch are catalogued in the MITRE ATT&CK framework, a knowledge base of the tactics and techniques observed in real-world intrusions. ATT&CK supplies the shared vocabulary in which defenders express what they can and cannot detect, and the associated practice of coverage measurement reports the proportion of relevant techniques for which at least one detection rule exists. Coverage is conventionally visualized as a matrix in which each technique is shaded according to whether a rule maps to it.
          A foundational insight of the field concerns the differing value of the observable characteristics on which a detection may be built. The Pyramid of Pain, introduced by David Bianco in 2013, orders indicator types by the cost imposed on an adversary who must change them in order to evade detection. File hashes and network addresses sit at the base, since an adversary can alter them at negligible cost; host and network artifacts occupy the middle; tools and behavioral tactics, techniques, and procedures occupy the apex, since changing these requires the adversary to alter how the intrusion is conducted. The practical consequence is that two rules may both detect the same technique while differing enormously in durability: one keyed to a specific file name is defeated by renaming a file, whereas one keyed to the underlying process behavior survives that change.
          This observation gives rise to a quality dimension distinct from coverage, commonly termed analytic robustness or evasion resistance. Coverage asks whether a technique is detected at all; robustness asks how much effort an adversary must expend to render that detection ineffective. The distinction matters because a coverage matrix treats all detections as equivalent, and therefore reports a technique as defended even when the defending rule can be evaded trivially. Formalizing robustness has been the subject of sustained work by the MITRE Engenuity Center for Threat-Informed Defense, whose Summiting the Pyramid (STP) methodology extends the conceptual ordering of the Pyramid of Pain into a structured scoring scheme. Under this methodology an analytic is examined to determine which observables its matching logic depends upon, and those observables are assigned to defined robustness levels for host-based and network-based detection, yielding an ordinal score for the analytic as a whole. Successive revisions of the methodology have added quantified robustness assessment and extended the scheme to network detections.
          The assessment of properties over declarative artifacts is a long-standing concern of computer science. Static analysis, developed principally for the examination of program source code without executing it, supplies the techniques of parsing a declarative artifact into a structured representation, traversing that representation to determine which inputs a computation depends upon, and reporting properties inferred from those dependencies. Dependency extraction over Boolean condition structures, feature extraction, and ordinal classification are established methods within this tradition. Where an automated classifier is intended to reproduce a judgment that human experts presently make, the statistical literature on inter-rater agreement supplies the appropriate validation instruments, of which Cohen's kappa coefficient is the most widely used, since it measures agreement between two raters while correcting for the agreement expected by chance alone. The application of these analytical and statistical methods to the artifacts of detection engineering forms the methodological context in which this study is situated.

The Current Process

          In current practice, the robustness of a detection rule is assessed by human judgment. A detection engineer reads the rule, identifies the fields on which its matching logic depends, and forms an opinion as to whether an adversary could evade the rule by altering those fields. This opinion draws on the engineer's accumulated experience of how adversaries behave and how easily particular artifacts can be changed. It is not recorded in the rule, is not expressed on any defined scale, and is not reviewable by a third party.
          A structured alternative exists in the form of the published Summiting the Pyramid methodology, which prescribes a defined procedure for determining the robustness level of an analytic. The procedure is, however, applied manually: an analyst works through it for one analytic at a time, examining the observables the analytic depends upon and assigning a level accordingly. Reflecting this cost, only a small proportion of the rules in the public Sigma corpus carry a manually assigned robustness annotation, while the remainder carry none.
          The automated validation available to detection content addresses other concerns entirely. Rule linters verify that a rule conforms to the expected schema, that required metadata fields are present, and that its ATT&CK mapping is well formed. Testing frameworks verify that a rule matches a sample of malicious log data and does not match a sample of benign data. None of these checks examines the durability of the rule against an adversary who modifies the observable it depends upon, and no available tool computes a robustness score automatically.
          The consequence is twofold. First, robustness remains unknown for the overwhelming majority of rules in use, so an organization cannot state how evadable its detection library is. Second, the coverage reporting on which security posture is communicated to management continues to treat every detection as equivalent, shading a technique as covered whether the covering rule is durable or trivially defeated. Detection engineers accordingly receive no systematic signal indicating which of their rules are brittle, nor which specific fields within a rule are responsible for that brittleness. This gap in the current process gives rise to the following problems.

Statement of the Problems

1. The robustness of a detection rule against adversary evasion is presently assessed manually and subjectively, on the basis of the individual engineer's experience, so assessments differ between engineers, are not expressed on a common scale, and cannot be reproduced or independently verified.
2. Applying the published structured scoring methodology requires an analyst to work through it one rule at a time, which does not scale to a corpus containing several thousand rules, with the result that the great majority of rules in operational use carry no robustness assessment at all.
3. Detection coverage reporting records a technique as covered whenever any rule maps to it, without regard to how easily that rule may be evaded, so published coverage figures systematically overstate the defensive capability they are taken to represent.
4. Because robustness is not computed, engineers receive no systematic indication of which specific observable fields are responsible for a rule's brittleness, and therefore no actionable guidance on how a rule might be rewritten to resist evasion more effectively.
5. There exists no automated means of assessing robustness whose output can be validated against an authoritative reference, so the accuracy of any scoring approach cannot presently be measured, and no evidence base exists on which to justify confidence in such an assessment.

Objectives of the Study

          General Objective. The study aims to design, develop, and evaluate a system that automatically computes the analytic robustness of Sigma and Wazuh detection rules by constructing a rule-feature dependency model, mapping the extracted observables to the robustness levels defined by the Summiting the Pyramid methodology, and validating the resulting scores against the manually assigned robustness annotations published in the Sigma corpus.

Specific Objectives

1. To design and implement a rule-feature dependency model that parses detection rules and extracts, through traversal of their Boolean condition structure, the set of observable fields on which each rule's matching logic depends, thereby establishing a reproducible and engineer-independent basis for robustness assessment. (addresses Problem 1)
2. To automate the assignment of each extracted observable to the robustness levels defined by the Summiting the Pyramid methodology and the computation of a per-rule robustness score, so that a corpus of several thousand rules can be scored without manual analysis. (addresses Problem 2)
3. To aggregate per-rule scores into per-technique robustness measures that qualify conventional coverage reporting, distinguishing techniques defended by evasion-resistant rules from those defended only by brittle ones. (addresses Problem 3)
4. To develop a recommendation component that identifies the specific observable fields limiting each rule's score and proposes substitutions drawn from the same event schema that would raise it, converting the assessment into actionable engineering guidance. (addresses Problem 4)
5. To evaluate the accuracy of the automated scoring by measuring its agreement with the manually assigned robustness annotations published in the Sigma corpus, using classification accuracy and Cohen's kappa coefficient, and to report the proportion of the corpus successfully parsed and scored. (addresses Problem 5)Project Context
Concept of the Proposed System

          The proposed system is a static analysis tool for detection content. It operates entirely offline on rule definition files, requiring no running monitoring platform, no log ingestion, and no network access during analysis. The system reads a corpus of detection rules, determines for each rule which observable fields its matching logic actually depends upon, maps those observables to the robustness levels prescribed by the published methodology, and computes a robustness score for the rule.
          The central contribution is that a judgment presently made by human experts, one rule at a time, is rendered computable. The system does not simply enumerate the fields mentioned in a rule, since a field appearing under a negation or within an alternative branch of a condition does not constrain the rule's matching behavior in the same way as a field the rule requires. It instead parses the rule into a structured condition representation and traverses that structure to establish genuine dependency before any scoring occurs. Scores are then aggregated to the technique level so that coverage reporting can be qualified by durability, and the observables limiting each score are identified so that the output constitutes engineering guidance rather than a bare measurement.
          The evaluation of the system rests on a favorable circumstance: a subset of the public rule corpus already carries robustness annotations assigned manually according to the same methodology. This subset constitutes an authoritative reference against which the automated scores can be compared, allowing accuracy to be measured rather than asserted.

Features and Capabilities

• Parsing of detection rules in the Sigma YAML format and in the Wazuh Extensible Markup Language rule format
• Normalization of heterogeneous rule syntax, including field modifiers, wildcards, value lists, and nested condition expressions, into a common structured representation
• Traversal of the Boolean condition structure to establish which observable fields a rule's matching behavior genuinely depends upon, distinguishing required conditions from alternatives and negations
• Resolution of log source declarations to determine the event schema to which each referenced field belongs
• Mapping of each resolved observable to its host or network robustness level using a maintained knowledge base derived from the published methodology
• Computation of a per-rule analytic robustness score under a defined aggregation rule
• Aggregation of per-rule scores into per-technique and per-tactic robustness measures
• Identification of the specific observable fields that limit each rule's score
• Recommendation of higher-level field substitutions drawn from the same event schema that would raise a rule's score
• Export of an ATT&CK Navigator layer shaded by robustness rather than by the presence or absence of coverage
• A validation mode computing classification accuracy, Cohen's kappa coefficient, and a confusion matrix against the manually annotated reference subset
• Reporting of analyzable coverage, expressed as the proportion of the corpus successfully parsed, resolved, and scored

System Modules

The features above are organized into five modules.

• Module 1 - Rule Ingestion and Parsing Module. This module reads the rule corpus and converts it into the internal representation used by the remainder of the system. It traverses the rule directory, parses Sigma rules expressed in YAML and Wazuh rules expressed in Extensible Markup Language, and normalizes their differing syntax into a common structured form comprising rule metadata, log source declaration, detection definitions, and the condition expression that combines them. It handles field modifiers, wildcards, and value lists, and it records rules that cannot be parsed rather than terminating the analysis, so that analyzable coverage can be reported as an explicit figure.
• Module 2 - Feature Dependency Extraction Module. This module constructs the rule-feature dependency model that gives the study its name. For each parsed rule it builds the Boolean condition tree implied by the rule's condition expression and traverses that tree to determine which observable fields the rule's matching behavior genuinely depends upon. The traversal distinguishes conjunctive dependencies, which the rule requires in order to match, from disjunctive alternatives and negated terms, which constrain matching differently and must not be scored as though they were requirements. It then resolves the log source declaration to identify the event schema in which each referenced field is defined, since the same field name may denote different observables under different schemas.
• Module 3 - Robustness Mapping and Scoring Module. This module contains the core algorithm of the study. It maintains a knowledge base that associates observables with the host and network robustness levels defined by the published methodology, and it assigns each extracted observable to its level through lookup against that knowledge base. It then computes a per-rule robustness score under a defined aggregation rule reflecting that a rule is only as durable as the weakest observable it requires, with explicit handling of the cases in which an observable cannot be resolved. Scores are subsequently aggregated to per-technique and per-tactic measures, and the observable responsible for limiting each rule's score is recorded.
• Module 4 - Recommendation and Substitution Module. This module converts measurement into guidance. For each rule whose score is limited by a low-level observable, it searches the event schema associated with that rule's log source for alternative fields that occupy a higher robustness level and that could plausibly express an equivalent detection intent. It presents these as candidate substitutions together with the score improvement each would yield, leaving the engineering judgment as to their validity with the analyst rather than applying them automatically.
• Module 5 - Validation, Reporting, and Visualization Module. This module presents results and supports the experimental evaluation of the study. It renders the ranked robustness report, the per-technique summary, and the ATT&CK Navigator layer shaded by robustness, and exports findings in machine-readable formats suitable for integration into a continuous integration pipeline. For evaluation it compares the automated scores against the manually annotated reference subset, computing classification accuracy, Cohen's kappa coefficient, and a confusion matrix showing the distribution of disagreements across robustness levels, together with the analyzable coverage of the corpus.

Activity Diagram of the Proposed System

          The activity diagram below uses four partitions representing the Security Detection Engineer, the proposed system, the rule corpus together with the robustness knowledge base, and the Detection Engineering Lead. The flow proceeds from the selection of a rule corpus through parsing, dependency extraction, schema resolution, and robustness mapping. Rules whose observables cannot be resolved are recorded as unscorable so that analyzable coverage is reported honestly rather than silently omitted. Scored rules proceed to aggregation, identification of limiting observables, and generation of the ranked report and Navigator layer. Rules judged improvable are rewritten using higher-level observables and returned to the corpus for re-scoring, while the aggregate robustness posture is reviewed for prioritization.


Importance of the Study

          To society. Organizations that safeguard personal, financial, and medical information report their security posture in terms of detection coverage, and management, regulators, and auditors rely on those reports in judging whether the organization is adequately defended. When coverage counts a trivially evadable rule as equivalent to a durable one, the resulting assurance is partly illusory, and defensive investment may be directed away from the areas where it is most needed. By rendering durability measurable alongside coverage, the study contributes to the accuracy of the claims on which the protection of citizens' data ultimately rests. The benefit accrues particularly to organizations that depend on openly published rule content because commercial detection libraries are beyond their means, a category that includes many small and medium enterprises, academic institutions, and public sector agencies in the Philippines.
          To the computer science and information technology security industry. The study delivers automated scoring for a methodology whose authors identified automation as outstanding work, and for which no public tool presently exists. Because the analysis is static and offline, it can be incorporated directly into the continuous integration pipeline through which rule contributions are reviewed, allowing a robustness threshold to be enforced at the point of contribution rather than assessed after deployment. The approach also supplies the industry with a means of qualifying coverage metrics, shifting the reporting of detection posture from a binary account of what is covered toward a graded account of how durably it is covered.
          To computer science as a discipline. The study applies static analysis and dependency extraction, methods developed for the examination of program source code, to declarative security artifacts that have received little formal treatment. It formalizes analytic robustness, presently an expert judgment, as a computable property derived from the dependency structure of a rule, and it demonstrates the validation of an automated classifier against expert human labels using inter-rater agreement statistics rather than accuracy alone. The resulting scored corpus and its comparison against the annotated reference subset provide reproducible evaluation material of a kind that work on detection content quality has generally lacked.
          To security operations practitioners. For detection engineers, the study converts an assessment that is currently performed on a handful of rules into one that executes across an entire library, and it returns not merely a score but the specific field responsible for limiting it together with candidate replacements. For analysts and threat hunters, the resulting annotations indicate which detections should not be relied upon in isolation, informing both alert triage and the design of hunting hypotheses intended to compensate for brittle coverage.
          To future researchers. The rule-feature dependency model, the observable knowledge base, and the validated scoring pipeline provide a foundation on which subsequent work can build, including extension to additional rule languages and event schemas, incorporation of further quality dimensions beyond robustness, empirical study of how robustness varies across techniques and contributors, and investigation of automated rule repair that applies substitutions rather than proposing them.Target Users

YOU CANNOT ESCAPE ME: DETECTING EVASIONS OF SIEM RULES IN ENTERPRISE NETWORKS
Uetz, Rafael; Herzog, Marco; Hackländer, Louis; Schwarz, Simon; Henze, Martin (2024)

August 2024, 33rd USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security 24), Philadelphia, PA, pp. 5179-5196, ISBN 978-1-939133-44-1; preprint arXiv:2311.10197.

https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity24/presentation/uetz

          This study addresses detection blind spots created when adversaries make small changes to their activity that evade expert-written SIEM detection rules while still achieving the same malicious effect. The authors first manually analyzed a subset of the public Sigma rule corpus and found that adversaries can easily evade almost half of widely used rules ("adversaries can evade almost half of them easily"), specifically about half of the roughly 300 analyzed Windows process-creation Sigma rules within the corpus. They then proposed adaptive misuse detection and built AMIDES, an open-source proof-of-concept that uses supervised machine learning trained on existing Sigma rules versus historical benign events to flag likely evasions and to attribute which rule an adversary attempted to bypass. Evaluation used four weeks of SIEM events from a large enterprise network plus more than 500 hand-crafted evasions, and AMIDES detected exactly 70 percent of evasions (358 true positives, 154 false negatives) with zero false alerts despite a data set containing roughly 145,000 times as many benign as malicious events (74,431,740 true negatives), while remaining computationally efficient. The work establishes empirically that a rule can be nominally present yet trivially defeated, which is the exact quality dimension the proposed project seeks to measure statically.

          This study is the strongest motivational anchor for the proposal because it demonstrates, with hard numbers on real Sigma rules, that coverage does not imply durability, which is precisely the gap between coverage and analytic robustness the proposal targets. Both works operate on the SigmaHQ corpus and both care about evasion resistance. The key difference is method and posture: AMIDES is a runtime, telemetry-driven, machine-learning detector that needs live benign events and an operating SIEM to catch evasions after the fact, whereas the proposal is an offline static analyzer that scores a rule's evasion resistance from its condition logic alone, with no logs, no training data, and no running SIEM.

HOW DOES ENDPOINT DETECTION USE THE MITRE ATT&CK FRAMEWORK?
Virkud, Apurva; Inam, Muhammad Adil; Riddle, Andy; Liu, Jason; Wang, Gang; Bates, Adam (2024)

August 2024, Proceedings of the 33rd USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security 24); ACM Digital Library entry DOI 10.5555/3698900.3699118.

https://www.usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity24-virkud.pdf

          This paper critically examines whether MITRE ATT&CK technique "coverage" is a valid metric for security posture. The authors analyzed three ATT&CK-annotated commercial detection rulesets (Carbon Black, Splunk, Elastic) and the crowdsourced Sigma ruleset to measure how products use ATT&CK and where the matrix is under-covered, then performed a qualitative analysis of unimplemented techniques to judge whether they are even realizable as detection rules, and finally a consistency analysis of ATT&CK labeling across products for 37 threat entities. They found that commercial products cover only 48 percent to 55 percent of the 191 techniques in enterprise ATT&CK v11 (Sigma's crowdsourced coverage was higher at 79 percent), that 53 techniques (27.7 percent) were not implemented in any commercial product, and that coverage drops to 25 percent to 26 percent (roughly half) once low- and medium-risk rules are filtered out. The central conclusion is that overreliance on ATT&CK coverage systematically overstates defensive capability, since a technique marked "covered" may be covered weakly or inconsistently. This is the empirical basis for the proposal's claim that coverage must be qualified by a robustness dimension. 

          The reviewed study and the proposal share the thesis that binary coverage reporting is misleading and needs a quality qualifier, and both study the Sigma corpus and ATT&CK mappings directly. The difference is that Virkud et al. critique coverage and analyze labeling consistency but stop short of producing a per-rule, reproducible robustness score; the proposal supplies exactly that missing ordinal measure and an ATT&CK Navigator layer shaded by robustness rather than by presence or absence.

STATIC QUALITY ASSESSMENT OF SIGMA DETECTION RULES: FRAMEWORK AND EMPIRICAL EVALUATION
Tyagi, Nishant (2026)

Written May 24, 2026; posted on SSRN June 5, 2026 (last revised June 6, 2026), 24 pages; DOI 10.2139/ssrn.6823718; archival deposit DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20371761.

https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6823718

          This is the closest existing work to the proposal's tooling design. It presents a multi-dimensional framework for STATIC detection-rule quality assessment, implemented in an open-source tool called sigmalint, and evaluates it against the full public SigmaHQ corpus of 3,132 rules at a pinned commit (994da16). The framework deliberately separates validity (a binary spec-conformance gate) from quality (a weighted score across six dimensions: ATT&CK alignment, taxonomy correctness, false-positive risk, metadata completeness, redundancy, and style), and it is deterministic given pinned reference data. Findings include that every SigmaHQ rule passes the validity gate, the corpus mean static-quality score is 99.18 out of 100, and two rule IDs (META004 and FP003) account for 89.8 percent of all findings, with a third (FP004) pushing the top-3 total to 93.2 percent; a seeded-defect benchmark with nine mutation operators gives controlled recall evidence (0.993 mean target-rule recall across 450 in-scope mutations). Notably the author discloses a measurement-bias correction in one of the tool's own rules (a 99.3 percent false-positive rate in rule STY003) as a methodology check.

          This study validates the proposal's whole premise (static, reproducible, corpus-scale scoring of Sigma rules with a validity/quality split) and even anticipates the proposal's "analyzable coverage" honesty by pinning commits and separating parseability from quality. The decisive gap is the quality dimension itself: sigmalint scores correctness, false-positive risk, and metadata completeness, but it does NOT score analytic robustness or evasion resistance and does not model the condition tree to determine which observables matching genuinely depends on. The proposal fills that specific missing dimension and adds STP-based weakest-dependency aggregation, which sigmalint does not attempt.

          This study examines the cybersecurity readiness of Philippine State Universities and Colleges, whose management information systems face rising threats to student data and institutional assets. The principal challenges identified were user education, cloud security, information security strategy, and unsecured personal devices, and the authors propose a program logic model to guide cybersecurity planning, implementation, and assessment for CHED, DICT, and PASUC. Both authors are affiliated with Philippine institutions. Information security strategy emerging as a named challenge is directly relevant, since strategy is communicated through posture reporting.

          The connection to the proposed study is the reporting problem: this work shows that Philippine institutions struggle to formulate and communicate an information security strategy, and the proposal's core argument is that conventional coverage reporting overstates defensive capability by treating a trivially evadable rule as equivalent to a durable one. Both are concerned with the honesty of the claims on which institutional security decisions rest. The difference is that De Ramos and Esponilla identify strategy weakness qualitatively without any measurement instrument, whereas the proposed system produces a quantified, chance-corrected, validated robustness measure that qualifies coverage and can be defended to management and auditors.
